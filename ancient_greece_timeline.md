# Ancient Greece: A Century-by-Century Timeline

## Background: The Bronze Age (c. 3000–1200 BC)
Before "Ancient Greece" proper, two civilizations set the stage:
- **Minoan Crete** (c. 2700–1450 BC): Palace complex at **Knossos**, associated with the legend of King Minos and the Labyrinth. Ended by a combination of the Thera/Santorini eruption and later Mycenaean takeover.
- **Mycenaean Greece** (c. 1600–1100 BC): Warrior-palace culture centered on **Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos**. This is the world later mythologized in Homer's epics — the (probably legendary or semi-historical) **Trojan War** is traditionally dated c. 1250–1180 BC.
- Collapse c. 1200–1150 BC: part of the wider **Bronze Age Collapse**, alongside the fall of the Hittites and upheavals across the eastern Mediterranean. Palace administration and writing (Linear B) disappear.

## The Greek Dark Ages (c. 1100–800 BC)
- Loss of literacy, population decline, small scattered settlements.
- Development of **iron working**, and — near the end of the period — a new alphabet adapted from Phoenician script.
- Oral epic poetry keeps alive stories of the Mycenaean past, culminating in Homer.

## 8th Century BC — Emergence
- **Politics/society**: The **polis** (city-state) begins to take shape as the basic Greek political unit — Athens, Sparta, Corinth, Argos, Thebes among the earliest to consolidate.
- **776 BC**: Traditional founding date of the **Olympic Games** at Olympia.
- **Colonization** begins in earnest: Greek cities found colonies in Sicily, southern Italy ("Magna Graecia"), and the Black Sea coast (e.g., Syracuse founded by Corinth, c. 733 BC).
- **Writers/thinkers**: **Homer** (Iliad, Odyssey) and **Hesiod** (Theogony, Works and Days) — the foundational texts of Greek literature, composed/written down in this century.
- Sparta begins its distinctive military-social transformation; conflict with neighboring Messenia begins (First Messenian War, late 8th century).

## 7th Century BC — Tyrants and Lawgivers
- **Politics**: Rise of **tyrants** (autocratic rulers, often populist, who seized power outside traditional aristocratic succession) in cities like Corinth (**Cypselus**) and Sicyon.
- **Sparta**: Completes conquest of Messenia (Second Messenian War), creating the **helot** system that underpins its unique militarized society; the **Lycurgan reforms** (semi-legendary) shape Spartan constitution and the agoge training system.
- **Athens**: **Draco** issues Athens' first written law code (c. 621 BC) — famously harsh ("draconian").
- **Writers**: Lyric poets flourish — **Archilochus**, and the beginnings of the lyric tradition that leads into **Sappho** and **Alcaeus** on Lesbos (writing right at the edge of this century into the next).
- Continued colonization; Greek contact with Egypt (trading post at Naukratis) and the wider Mediterranean deepens.

## 6th Century BC — Reform and Philosophy
- **Athens**: **Solon** (c. 594 BC) enacts major economic and constitutional reforms, cancelling debt-bondage and reorganizing citizenship classes. Later, the tyrant **Pisistratus** and his sons rule Athens (c. 561–510 BC), sponsoring public works, festivals, and the codification of Homeric text. The tyranny falls in 510 BC; **Cleisthenes** then introduces reforms (508/507 BC) creating the foundations of Athenian **democracy** — reorganizing citizens into ten tribes and establishing the Council of 500.
- **Sparta** organizes the **Peloponnesian League**, becoming the dominant land power in Greece.
- **Persia**: Cyrus the Great and then Darius I build the Achaemenid Persian Empire, absorbing the Greek cities of Ionia (Asia Minor) — setting up later conflict.
- **Philosophy/science** — the "Pre-Socratics": **Thales of Miletus** (often called the first philosopher, predicted a solar eclipse), **Anaximander**, **Anaximenes**, **Pythagoras** (mathematics, mysticism, founds a community at Croton in Italy), **Xenophanes**, **Heraclitus** ("you cannot step in the same river twice").
- **Writers**: **Sappho** and **Alcaeus** (Lesbos, personal/lyric poetry); **Aesop** traditionally dated here (fables); early dramatic contests begin at Athens' City Dionysia (**Thespis**, traditionally the first actor, c. 534 BC).

## 5th Century BC — The Classical Peak
- **499–449 BC: The Persian Wars.**
  - 499 BC: Ionian Revolt against Persia (Athens sends aid).
  - 490 BC: **Battle of Marathon** — Athens (with Plataea) defeats Darius I's invasion force.
  - 480 BC: Xerxes I invades; **Battle of Thermopylae** (Leonidas and the 300 Spartans, delaying action) and the sack of Athens; **Battle of Salamis** — decisive Greek naval victory (largely Athenian fleet under Themistocles).
  - 479 BC: **Battle of Plataea** ends the Persian invasion on land.
- **Athens ascendant**: Forms the **Delian League** (477 BC), nominally a defensive alliance, which becomes an Athenian maritime empire funded by allied tribute. **Pericles** dominates Athenian politics from the 450s, overseeing the building of the **Parthenon** and other Acropolis monuments, and the flourishing of radical democracy.
- **431–404 BC: The Peloponnesian War** — Athens (and its empire) vs. Sparta (and the Peloponnesian League). Key episodes: the Athenian plague (430 BC, killed Pericles), the Sicilian Expedition (415–413 BC, catastrophic Athenian defeat), Spartan naval buildup with Persian funding, and final Athenian surrender in 404 BC. Sparta becomes briefly dominant in Greece.
- **Philosophy**: **Socrates** (c. 470–399 BC) teaches in Athens, questioning everything via the "Socratic method"; tried and executed in 399 BC. Sophists (Protagoras, Gorgias) teach rhetoric and relativism.
- **History writing**: **Herodotus** ("Father of History") writes his Histories on the Persian Wars; **Thucydides** writes his unflinching History of the Peloponnesian War.
- **Tragedy and comedy**: The three great tragedians — **Aeschylus** (Oresteia, Persians), **Sophocles** (Oedipus Rex, Antigone), **Euripides** (Medea, The Bacchae) — all working in Athens; comic playwright **Aristophanes** (Lysistrata, The Clouds, The Frogs) satirizes politics and society.
- **Art/architecture**: The sculptor **Phidias** oversees the Parthenon's sculptural program; classical sculpture (Polykleitos) establishes idealized human proportion.

## 4th Century BC — Hegemony, Philosophy, and Macedon
- **Politics/war**: No single city dominates for long. Sparta's harsh hegemony after 404 BC provokes the **Corinthian War** (395–387 BC). **Thebes** rises under the general **Epaminondas**, decisively breaking Spartan military supremacy at the **Battle of Leuctra** (371 BC) using innovative tactics; Theban dominance is brief, ending with Epaminondas's death at Mantinea (362 BC).
- **Rise of Macedon**: **Philip II of Macedon** (r. 359–336 BC) reforms the Macedonian army (the sarissa phalanx) and subdues the Greek city-states, culminating in victory at the **Battle of Chaeronea** (338 BC), effectively ending Greek city-state independence. The orator **Demosthenes** leads Athenian resistance to Macedon through his Philippics.
- **Alexander the Great** (r. 336–323 BC), Philip's son, conquers the Persian Empire (Battles of Granicus, Issus, Gaugamela), Egypt, and pushes into India, spreading Greek culture across the Near East before dying in Babylon in 323 BC. His empire fragments among his generals (the **Diadochi**), beginning the **Hellenistic period**.
- **Philosophy**: **Plato** (c. 428–348 BC) founds the **Academy** in Athens, writes the dialogues (Republic, Symposium, Phaedo). His student **Aristotle** (384–322 BC) founds the **Lyceum**, writes on logic, biology, ethics, politics, and metaphysics, and tutors the young Alexander.
- **Other writers/thinkers**: **Diogenes of Sinope** (Cynic philosophy, lived provocatively in a barrel); historian **Xenophon** (Anabasis, on the "March of the Ten Thousand").

## 3rd Century BC — The Hellenistic World
- Greek culture and language spread across a vast area following Alexander's conquests, though the political unity is gone. Three major successor kingdoms dominate:
  - **Ptolemaic Egypt**, capital **Alexandria** — becomes the intellectual capital of the Greek world, home to the **Library of Alexandria** and the Museum.
  - **Seleucid Empire**, spanning Mesopotamia to parts of Central Asia.
  - **Antigonid Macedon**, ruling Greece itself.
- Greek city-states form **federal leagues** (the Aetolian League, the Achaean League) to preserve some autonomy against the larger kingdoms.
- **Science/mathematics** flourishes in Alexandria: **Euclid** (Elements, geometry), **Archimedes** of Syracuse (mathematics, engineering, physics — though based in Sicily), **Eratosthenes** (calculated Earth's circumference, chief librarian at Alexandria).
- **Philosophy**: New schools respond to a world of large impersonal kingdoms rather than small city-states — **Epicurus** founds Epicureanism (pleasure/tranquility, atomism) in Athens; **Zeno of Citium** founds **Stoicism** in Athens.
- **Literature**: Hellenistic poetry becomes more scholarly and personal — **Callimachus**, **Apollonius of Rhodes** (Argonautica), Alexandrian court poetry.

## 2nd Century BC — Rome Intervenes
- **Rome** progressively conquers the Hellenistic world. The **Macedonian Wars** (214–148 BC) end Antigonid independence.
- **146 BC**: Rome destroys **Corinth**, crushing the Achaean League and effectively ending Greek political independence — mainland Greece becomes a Roman protectorate/province.
- Greek culture, however, deeply influences Rome in this period ("Graecia capta ferum victorem cepit" — captured Greece captured her rough conqueror, as the poet Horace later put it) — Greek philosophy, art, and literature are absorbed into elite Roman education.
- The Seleucid and Ptolemaic kingdoms persist but weaken, squeezed by Rome and internal strife.

## 1st Century BC — The Last Hellenistic Kingdom Falls
- Rome continues absorbing the remaining Hellenistic territories: the Seleucid Empire collapses and becomes the Roman province of Syria (64 BC, under Pompey).
- **Ptolemaic Egypt** survives longest as a nominally independent Hellenistic kingdom, ruled by the Greek-descended Ptolemaic dynasty, ending with **Cleopatra VII**.
- **31 BC: Battle of Actium** — Octavian (later Augustus) defeats Mark Antony and Cleopatra.
- **30 BC**: Egypt is annexed by Rome following Cleopatra's death — conventionally marks the **end of the Hellenistic period** and of "Ancient Greece" as an independent political entity, though Greek language, culture, philosophy, and identity remain vibrant within the Roman (and later Byzantine) world for centuries afterward.

### A note on periodization
Historians divide this span differently depending on focus:
- **Archaic period**: c. 800–480 BC
- **Classical period**: c. 480–323 BC
- **Hellenistic period**: 323–30 BC

This timeline groups things by century for readability, but keep those broader labels in mind — they're the terms you'll most often see used in books and museum labels.
